## In short

**Keśava Kāśmīrī**, the "Digvijayī" (conqueror of all directions), a great paṇḍita of the **Nimbārka sampradāya** from Kashmir, came to Navadvīpa after defeating scholars all over India and was defeated by the young **Nimāi Paṇḍita**; by Sarasvatī's mercy he understood who the Lord was and surrendered to Him (*Caitanya-bhāgavata*, Ādi 13; *Caitanya-caritāmṛta*, Ādi 16). The *Bhakti-ratnākara* gives his line from Nārāyaṇa through Haṁsa, the four Kumāras, Nārada and Nimbāditya down to Keśava Bhaṭṭa, Gokula Bhaṭṭa and Keśava Kāśmīrī (12.2243-2276).

## The Digvijayī

He was learned in nyāya, poetry, rhetoric and grammar, and a devotee of **Sarasvatī**, the goddess of learning, whom he pleased by chanting her mantra; she gave him the benediction to conquer all scholars. All the scriptures were on the tip of his tongue; no one could challenge him, many could not even follow his introductions. Hearing of Navadvīpa's fame as a city of scholars, he came there in great pomp with elephants, horses and men, and waves of fear went through every house.

## Defeated by Nimāi

One evening on the bank of the Ganges, the boy Nimāi, a teacher of grammar, asked him to describe the Ganges. He composed a hundred verses on the spot; the Lord quoted one of them back and pointed out its faults of rhetoric, and the Digvijayī, defeated by a boy, was speechless. That night Sarasvatī appeared to him in a dream and told him that the young teacher was the Supreme Lord, her own master. In the morning he came to the Lord, fell at His feet and gave up his pride; the Lord blessed him with devotion and sent him to preach (*Caitanya-bhāgavata*; *Caitanya-caritāmṛta* Ādi 16). The record also tells, from Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura's *Navadvīpa-dhāma-māhātmya*, how long before His appearance the Lord told Nimbārka in Navadvīpa that he would be born in Kashmir as Keśava Kāśmīrī, that the Lord would defeat him in Māyāpura, and that by Sarasvatī's mercy he would know Him, give up his pride and preach.

## Writings

He wrote the *Laghu-keśava*, the *Vedānta-kaustubha-prabhā*, the *Tattva-prakāśikā* (a commentary on the Gītā), the *Govinda-śaraṇāgati-stotra* and the *Yamunā-stotra*; in the maṅgalācaraṇa of the *Kaustubha-prabhā* and the *Tattva-prakāśikā* he bows to **Mukunda** and **Gaṅgala Bhaṭṭa** as his gurus. A manuscript *Dhucakra-digvijayī* in his name was found at the Salimabad gaddī. Some wrongly take him to be Keśavācārya, the author of the *Krama-dīpikā*.
