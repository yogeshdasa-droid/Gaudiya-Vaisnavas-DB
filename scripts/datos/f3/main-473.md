## In short

**Haridāsa Sādhu** was a famous yogī of the early nineteenth century in the Punjab, known in India and abroad for staying alive for weeks and months buried in the earth in samādhi. He was born in a village of **Mahārāṣṭra**; at fifteen or sixteen he followed a sannyāsī from Tailaṅga-deśa, a Vaiṣṇava of the "Kuver" sect, and was initiated by him at **Puṣkara**. He is not of the Gauḍīya line (his record stands in the branch «Other sampradāyas»).

## Yoga

At **Kurukṣetra** with his guru he learned the hard disciplines of yoga — the rules of eating, āsana-bandhana, control of speech and prāṇāyāma — and the **khecarī-mudrā**, turning the tongue back to hold the breath for samādhi; he gained powers that won him the respect of rājās and ministers, Sunni Muslims and even Christians hostile to Hinduism.

## The burials

By 1815 his fame had spread: at **Amritsar** he had stayed alive four months under mounds of earth, and at **Jammu**, before Rājā **Dhyān Siṁha**, the minister of Mahārājā **Ranjit Siṁha**, he lay four months under the earth — clean-shaven when he went in, with no beard when he came out. When Lords Bentinck and Auckland asked their political agents to check the reports, **McNaughton** brought him to Puṣkara and kept him locked in a safe in his own room for thirteen days; he was found stiff as dry wood, and soon came back to life. For the childless Mahārāval of **Galsamir** he lay a month in a pit three feet long, before Lieutenant Baylow and other officials. At **Lahore** in **1835**, at the wedding of Nau Nihāl Siṁha, Ranjit Siṁha, doubting, had him locked in a sealed wooden box, buried in the palace courtyard, and barley sown over it; after forty days, when the barley had grown, he was dug up before Captain Wade, declared dead by Drs McGregor and Murray — and revived by his disciples. Once more, at Ranjit Siṁha's request, he stayed nearly ten months under the ground; that was his last demonstration.

People said many things of him: that he was a Frenchman come to the Punjab after Waterloo; that he was Vedavyāsa, hidden under the earth since the beginning of Kali-yuga and dug up by the English; the Sikhs held him to be Guru Nānak. To the English he explained three rules — prāṇāyāma, khecarī-mudrā and the rules of eating — by which the body becomes inert like a corpse; the bliss of samādhi was such that he did not feel it as a penance, only the preparations were painful. Insulted at the Rāṇī of Jhindan's advice, he cursed the Mahārājā to have no descendants and vanished from Lahore with his disciples. Announcing that this time no one would revive him, he sat in samādhi and passed away. In his day, says the *Viśvakoṣa*, when missionaries were saying that the Hindu śāstras had no substance, his powers showed what their practices could achieve.
