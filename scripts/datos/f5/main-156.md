## In short

**Bilvamaṅgala Ṭhākura**, called **Līlāśuka**, is the author of the **Śrī Kṛṣṇa-karṇāmṛta**, the book Śrī Caitanya Mahāprabhu found in South India and relished day and night in the Gambhīrā (*Caitanya-caritāmṛta*, Madhya 9.307-308; Antya 15.27, 20.67-68). Born in a brāhmaṇa family on the bank of the **Kṛṣṇa-veṇṇā** in South India, he was saved from a life of lust by the prostitute **Cintāmaṇi**, initiated by **Somagiri**, and lived long in Vṛndāvana, where Kṛṣṇa Himself came to him. The *Śrī Vallabha-digvijaya* makes him the chief disciple of **Viṣṇusvāmī**, in the eighth century Śaka.

## Cintāmaṇi

He was a learned brāhmaṇa, madly attached to Cintāmaṇi, a dancing girl and harlot who lived on the other bank of the river. One stormy night — the very day of his father's śrāddha — finding no boat, he crossed the raging river holding on to a corpse; finding her gate bolted, he climbed the wall holding a snake he took for a rope, and fell senseless inside. She nursed him, and then said: "You are so attached to this bag of flesh and bones; had you loved God as you love me, you would be a saint. Better serve the blissful Govinda; go to Vṛndāvana." Her words changed his life; she too renounced the world, and he honours her in the first verse of the *Kṛṣṇa-karṇāmṛta* as the first guru who showed him the way.

## Somagiri and Līlāśuka

The next day he renounced the world and was initiated by the sannyāsī **Somagiri** (at Somagiri, says one text), who taught him self-control and gave him the Gopāla-mantra. At first an impersonalist, meditating on the brahman, he became a devotee, as he himself writes in a verse Rūpa Gosvāmī quotes: "Though I was worshipped by those on the path of monism and initiated into self-realisation, I have been forcibly turned into a maidservant by a cunning boy who sports with the gopīs" (*Bhakti-rasāmṛta-sindhu* 3.1.44). Seeing the verses on Kṛṣṇa's Vṛndāvana-līlā he composed while serving him, Somagiri named him **Līlāśuka**, "the parrot of the pastimes", and let him go to Vṛndāvana. On the way he once lusted after a brāhmaṇa's wife; disgusted with himself, he pierced his own eyes with her hairpins.

## Vṛndāvana

Blind, he went on singing, seeing Kṛṣṇa with spiritual vision. In Vṛndāvana Kṛṣṇa, as a cowherd boy, brought him milk and prasāda and led him to a safe place to rest; when the boy played His flute he understood who He was, reached out and touched His hand — and the boy laughed and ran away. "You can run away from my hand, but You can never leave my heart." In the *Kṛṣṇa-karṇāmṛta* he names Kṛṣṇa, the cowherd boy with the peacock feather, as his śikṣā-guru, and bows equally to Cintāmaṇi, Somagiri and Him. He is said to have lived seven hundred years near **Brahma-kuṇḍa** in Vṛndāvana; his samādhi is in Gopīnātha Bāzār. Another tradition makes him the founder of the Dvārakādhīśa temple at Dvārakā and the first worshipper of Padmanābha at Trivandrum; his memory is still alive at Trichur in Kerala.

## The Kṛṣṇa-karṇāmṛta

"Nectar for the ears", it was one of the first books to reveal Śrīmatī Rādhārāṇī's supreme position in Kṛṣṇa's mādhurya-līlā, an idea the six Gosvāmīs, Rāmānanda Rāya, Prabodhānanda, Kṛṣṇadāsa Kavirāja, Viśvanātha and Bhaktivinoda later expanded. Mahāprabhu heard its first śataka (112 verses) recited by brāhmaṇa Vaiṣṇavas on the bank of the Kṛṣṇā, had it copied with great care, and told Rāmānanda Rāya that His own principle of devotion was the same; Rāmānanda Rāya, Vāsudeva Datta and others made copies. Kṛṣṇadāsa Kavirāja wrote the commentary *Sāraṅga-raṅgadā*, which tells the poet's life, and says there is no book like it in the three worlds. He also wrote the *Śrī Govinda-dāmodara-stotra*.
