## In short

Śrī **Jayadeva Gosvāmī**, the poet of the **Śrī Gīta-govinda**, was born at **Kendubilva** (Kenduli) in the Birbhum district, on the Ajaya river, in the eleventh or twelfth century, the son of **Bhojadeva** and **Vāmā Devī**; he was the court paṇḍita of King **Lakṣmaṇa Sena** of Gauḍa (Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇava Abhidhāna). About three hundred years before Śrī Caitanya, he sang the intimate pastimes of Rādhā and Govinda, and Mahāprabhu relished his songs day and night with Svarūpa Dāmodara and Rāmānanda Rāya (*Caitanya-caritāmṛta*, Madhya 2). His disappearance is on the **kṛṣṇa ṣaṣṭhī of Pauṣa** (Gauḍīya calendar); at Kenduli a great fair, the **Jayadeva-melā**, is held every year at Pauṣa-saṅkrānti (1 Māgha).

## Navadvīpa and Purī

He lived long in Navadvīpa near the palace of Lakṣmaṇa Sena, whose chief scholar was **Govardhana Ācārya**; in the *Gīta-govinda* he names his friends there, Umāpatidhara, Govardhana Ācārya and the poet Kṣamāpati. When the king heard his **Daśāvatāra-stotra** he came to his house in disguise and invited him to the palace; Jayadeva, a renunciant, preferred to go to Purī, so the king built him a cottage at **Campahāṭī** (Campaka-haṭṭa), where he had a vision of Rādhā-Mādhava and then of Their united form, the golden Gaurāṅga, who told him to go to Purī: there, after taking sannyāsa, He would Himself relish the *Gīta-govinda* (Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura, *Navadvīpa-dhāma-māhātmya*). Other sources make him the court poet of the king of Orissa (*Alaṅkāra-śekhara*); his verses are in Śrīdhara dāsa's *Sad-ukti-karṇāmṛta*. At Purī, says the *Bhakti-māhātmya* and the *Bhakta-mālā*, he served Puruṣottama and gave dīkṣā to some disciples.

## Padmāvatī

A childless brāhmaṇa who worshipped Jagannātha was given a daughter, **Padmāvatī**; when she came of age he offered her to the Lord, and Jagannātha told him: "My servant Jayadeva has given up his home to chant My names; give your daughter to him." Jayadeva refused, but the brāhmaṇa left her with him; she wept: "By Jagannātha's order my father gave me to you; you are my husband and my all. If you do not accept me I shall die at your feet." So the poet became a householder, and installed a Deity of Nārāyaṇa — later his **Rādhā-Mādhava**, found, it is said, in the Ajaya river.

## "Dehi pada-pallavam udāram"

Writing the *Gīta-govinda*, he came to a line where Kṛṣṇa bows to touch Śrī Rādhā's feet, and his hand shrank: how could he write that of the Supreme Lord? He went to bathe, leaving Padmāvatī cooking for the Deities. Kṛṣṇa came in Jayadeva's form, wrote the line *dehi pada-pallavam udāram* in his manuscript, ate the prasāda she served, and left. When Jayadeva returned and heard what had happened, he found the verse in wet ink, wept, and told her: "You are most fortunate: Kṛṣṇa Himself has written it and taken prasāda from your hand."

Other stories are told: the Lord Jagannātha came, covered with dust and thorns, to hear a gardener woman sing the *Gīta-govinda* in her garden, and her descendants still sing it in the temple; the king of Orissa wrote a rival *Gīta-govinda*, the Lord threw it away and kept Jayadeva's, but promised that twelve of the king's verses would be sung with it; Hari Himself helped him repair his roof in the summer heat. Robbers once cut off his hands and feet and threw him into a well, where a king heard him chanting Kṛṣṇa's name; when the same robbers came to the palace dressed as Vaiṣṇavas, Jayadeva gave them money and had them escorted away — and they fell dead when they slandered him: "One should be kind even to the wicked." To test Padmāvatī's words that a wife dies with her husband, the queen spread a false report of his death; Padmāvatī died at once, and Jayadeva brought her back to life with the holy name in her ear.

## Vṛndāvana and Kenduli

He took Rādhā-Mādhava to **Vṛndāvana** and served Them near **Keśī-ghāṭa**, where a merchant built Them a temple; after him the King of Jaipur took the Deities to **Ghāṭī** near Jaipur, where They are still served. It is said he spent his last years at Kenduli, walking daily to the Ganges far away, until one day, when he could not go, Gaṅgā Devī came to Kenduli for him. Some say he left this world at Kenduli, some at Purī — Bhaktisiddhānta Sarasvatī's opinion — some in Vṛndāvana.

## His work

Besides the *Gīta-govinda* — whose **Daśāvatāra-stotra** is famous — he is said to have written the *Candrāloka*. The *Gīta-govinda* has been translated into Hindi, Bengali, Oriyā, Assamese and many other languages and has more than twenty commentaries (Viśvakoṣa). Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura writes that although Caṇḍīdāsa, Vidyāpati, Bilvamaṅgala and Jayadeva lived before Mahāprabhu appeared, His conception of bhakti had arisen in their hearts. Vanamālī dāsa, a disciple of Śrīnivāsa, wrote a *Jayadeva-caritra*.
